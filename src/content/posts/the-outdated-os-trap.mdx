## **The Glitch Everyone Feels but Nobody Names**

Walk into any lecture hall on campus. Look around. You'll see two types of students:

- The ones taking furious notes, transcribing every slide as if the words themselves contain magic.
- The ones scrolling, half-listening, waiting for the recording to be posted.

Both groups are running on the same unspoken assumption: that showing up and absorbing is enough.

It's not.

The university is a remarkable institution. It's also an institution optimised for a world where information was scarce, gatekept, and slow-moving. That world is gone. The syllabus you're handed on day one was likely approved by a committee two years ago. By the time you're tested on it, the industry you're preparing for has already moved on.

This isn't a conspiracy. It's just inertia. And inertia doesn't care about your career.

Here's the uncomfortable truth:

> *The system isn't going to update itself in time for you.*

That doesn't make the system evil. It just means the responsibility for upgrading your own mental operating system has shifted. It's no longer centralised in the institution. It's distributed. And the node that matters most is&nbsp;**you**.

## **Learning Debt: The Interest Rate Is Higher Than You Think**

I think about learning the way I think about software systems. Every concept you "know" is a module. Some modules are well-tested, robust, and deeply understood. Others are patches you crammed the night before an exam; functional under narrow conditions, but guaranteed to fail under real-world load.

The gap between what a grade says you know and what you can actually&nbsp;**do**&nbsp;when the conditions change is what I call&nbsp;**learning debt**.

And just like technical debt, it compounds silently. You pass the course. You move on. The cracks don't show up until you're in a situation that demands synthesis, not recall.

I've watched this happen to people I respect. Someone with a near-perfect GPA, unable to debug a simple race condition because the textbook example didn't include a network delay. Someone who aced their machine learning final, stumped by a messy, unlabeled dataset from a real customer.

The problem isn't intelligence. The problem is that they were trained on&nbsp;**clean data**. The world runs on&nbsp;**dirty data**.

So how do you start paying down that debt? How do you upgrade your own OS while still meeting the demands of the old one?

## **The Four Stages of Competence (Hacked for the AI Era)**

This framework has been around since the 1970s

[^1]

[^1]: Broadwell, M. M. (1969). *Teaching for Learning (XVI)*. Burch, N. (1970s). *Four Stages for Learning Any New Skill*.

. It's not new. But the way we can use it&nbsp;*now*—with the tools we have&nbsp;*now*—changes everything. Here's how I've come to see each stage.

---

---

### **Stage 1: Unconsciously Unskilled — The Syllabus Blindspot**

**What it feels like:**&nbsp;"This class is straightforward. I'm on track."\
**What's actually happening:**&nbsp;The syllabus defines your boundaries. It tells you what to&nbsp;*exclude*. And what it excludes is often the most valuable terrain.

**The Pattern I've Observed:**&nbsp;Students who follow the syllabus perfectly tend to graduate perfectly prepared for a world that existed three years ago. Students who read&nbsp;**around**&nbsp;the syllabus—who chase questions the professor didn't assign—end up with a map that extends beyond the campus gates.

**The Adjacent Possible:**&nbsp;This is a concept borrowed from biology and complexity theory. The most interesting new ideas don't emerge from the centre of a discipline. They emerge at the&nbsp;**edges**, where fields collide. Computer science meets ethics. Biology meets information theory. Economics meets behavioural psychology.

**Your Upgrade:**\
Don't ask AI to help you with your homework. Ask it to be a&nbsp;**radar**&nbsp;for the adjacent possible. Try this prompt once a month:

> *"I'm studying [your major]. What are three emerging, slightly controversial, or fringe topics at the intersection of my field and another that will likely reshape my industry in five years? Give me one example of a problem that doesn't exist in textbooks yet."*

If the answer doesn't make you a little uncomfortable about what you&nbsp;*aren't*&nbsp;learning, you're not looking far enough.

### **Stage 2: Consciously Unskilled — The Moment Most People Quit**

**What it feels like:**&nbsp;"I have read this paragraph four times and I still don't know what it means. I must be stupid."\
**What's actually happening:**&nbsp;You've hit the edge of your current understanding. This is the&nbsp;**download bar**. It's not a sign to stop. It's a sign that you're installing new capacity.

**The Pattern I've Observed:**&nbsp;The students who thrive in the long run are not the ones who understand everything quickly. They're the ones who have a higher tolerance for the&nbsp;**friction**&nbsp;of not knowing. They've learned to sit in the discomfort and ask better questions.

**The Question Trap:**&nbsp;When we hit friction, we tend to ask,&nbsp;*"Why don't I get this?"*&nbsp;Research suggests that "why" questions often backfire

[^2]

[^2]: Kross, E., & Ayduk, O. (2017). *Self-Distancing: Theory, Research, and Current Directions*. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology.

. They pull us toward fixed explanations ("I'm not a math person") and stir negative emotion. A more productive shift is to ask&nbsp;*"What?"*:

- *What*&nbsp;specific part is breaking my understanding?
- *What*&nbsp;assumption am I making that might be false?
- *What*&nbsp;smaller piece can I grasp first?

**Your Upgrade:**\
Use AI not as an answer key, but as a&nbsp;**friction translator**. Prompt:

> *"Explain [concept] in three layers: like I'm 12, like I'm a peer, and like I'm an expert. For each layer, point out the one thing people usually get wrong."*

This doesn't give you the answer. It gives you a scaffold to climb up.

### **Stage 3: Consciously Skilled — The Temptation to Cheat Yourself**

**What it feels like:**&nbsp;"I can do this, but I have to focus. It's slow."\
**What's actually happening:**&nbsp;You are building&nbsp;**myelin**. You are literally changing the physical structure of your brain. This is where shortcuts are most dangerous.

**The Pattern I've Observed:**&nbsp;The rise of code-generation AI has created a new kind of learning debt. It's possible to produce working code, a coherent essay, or a solved problem set without ever&nbsp;**activating the neural circuitry**&nbsp;required for deep understanding. The output looks good. The grade is fine. But the mental model remains fragile.

The rule I've set for myself—and the one I think is safest—is simple:&nbsp;**AI can critique my thinking, but it cannot originate it.**

Write the first draft. Build the first version. Form the argument in your own head.&nbsp;*Then*&nbsp;hand it over with a prompt like:

> *"Here is my reasoning. Tear it apart. Where is the logic weak? What am I overconfident about? What would break this?"*

If you're not willing to be wrong in private, you will be exposed in public.

### **Stage 4: Unconsciously Skilled — The Ceiling Disguised as a View**

**What it feels like:**&nbsp;"I don't even think about this anymore. It's easy."\
**What's actually happening:**&nbsp;You've automated a skill. Congratulations. Now,&nbsp;**beware**. This is where growth quietly dies.

An 'A' on a transcript is not a destination. It's a signal that you've solved a&nbsp;**local optimisation problem**&nbsp;under&nbsp;**fixed constraints**. The real world will change the constraints without warning you.

**The Pattern I've Observed:**&nbsp;The people who get stuck after college are often the ones who were&nbsp;*best*&nbsp;at being students. They mastered the game of the classroom so thoroughly that they forgot how to play without a rubric.

**Your Upgrade:**&nbsp;Identify the skills that are now automatic. Deliberately&nbsp;**break the automation**. Find a harder problem in the same domain. Change the conditions. What happens if you have to do it without your usual tools? What happens if you have to explain it to someone who knows nothing?

Some skills deserve obsession. Others should be allowed to run in the background. Knowing which is which is not a talent. It's a practice.

## **The Debug Protocol: What to Do When You Crash**

You will bomb a test. You will misjudge a project. You will freeze in an interview. I have done all three.

When it happens, the standard student response is to&nbsp;**brute-force harder**. More hours. More flashcards. More caffeine. That's just panic with a highlighter.

I use a simple&nbsp;**APR Protocol**:

- **Awareness:**&nbsp;Write down exactly what failed. Not "I'm bad at math." That's useless. "I couldn't apply the chain rule because I was missing a core derivative rule." That's actionable.
- **Pause:**&nbsp;Do not immediately open the textbook. Walk away for 20 minutes. Let your brain switch modes. Friction demands analysis, not more friction.
- **Reframe:**&nbsp;Say this out loud:&nbsp;*"This failure is not a verdict on my intelligence. It is user feedback on my current learning system."*

Your job isn't to feel better. It's to patch the bug.

## **The Real Point of a Degree (The Part Nobody Says Out Loud)**

A degree might get you the interview. It will not make you indispensable. It will not make you resilient. It will not make you curious.

That part is on you.

The future doesn't care about the classes you took. It cares about the problems you can&nbsp;**notice**&nbsp;and the speed at which you can&nbsp;**upgrade yourself**&nbsp;to solve them.

Don't just be a student of your discipline. Be a researcher of your own potential.

And for the love of everything, stop waiting for the lecture hall to catch up. The OS update is yours to run.
